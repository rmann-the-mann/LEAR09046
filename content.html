Introduction

This portfolio supports an application for Recognition of Prior Learning (RPL) for LEAR09046, Developing Practice in Learning, Teaching and Assessment Online.

As Academic Leader, Digital Learning and Faculty Development at SAIT, and previously as a Faculty Development Facilitator, I have supported instructors across multiple disciplines in the effective use of educational technologies, online learning design, learner engagement strategies, assessment practices, and faculty development initiatives.

The evidence presented demonstrates learning acquired through the design, development, and implementation of PATO (Preparing to Teach Online), a professional learning program for SAIT instructors. The portfolio is organized around the module’s eight learning outcomes, with evidence drawn directly from this work.

Evidence Timeline

The evidence spans more than one stage of PATO's development. Some artifacts, the self-study, impact infographic, learner profile survey, lesson plans, and recommendations report, come from earlier versions. Others show the current Brightspace course structure. PATO wasn't a one-time build. It evolved through design, delivery, feedback, evaluation, and revision. Much of the earlier evidence captures the institutional learning that shaped each subsequent iteration.

Learning Outcome 1

Critically discuss learning theories and pedagogical approaches as they relate to online and blended learning, teaching, and assessment.

PATO's design was shaped by several complementary learning theories; the key was using them together rather than picking one. The Community of Inquiry (CoI) framework (Garrison et al., 2000) was the most significant influence. CoI argues that meaningful online learning emerges through the intersection of social, teaching, and cognitive presence. In PATO, I addressed social presence through introductions, community guidelines (E06), and peer interaction; teaching presence through course organization, facilitation, and feedback; and cognitive presence through reflection, discussion, and application (E07, E08, E09). This framework pushed me past simply adding interaction and toward asking what each interaction was actually supposed to contribute to learning.

The course also draws on social constructivist principles, particularly the view that learning is socially mediated (Vygotsky, 1978), and on principles of andragogy (Knowles et al., 2020). Because participants came in as experienced professionals, activities were deliberately connected to real teaching challenges rather than abstract concepts. No single theory addressed everything that was needed when designing PATO. I found it more useful to draw on different theories depending on the design decision I was making rather than trying to apply one framework across the entire course.

One important limitation I discovered: visible interaction isn't the same as learning. Participating in a poll or community activity can support social presence without necessarily producing cognitive engagement or transfer. This pushed me to think more carefully about how prompts, facilitation, reflection, and assessment connect interaction to actual outcomes (Rourke & Kanuka, 2009).

Evidence Referenced: E06, E07, E08, E09

Learning Outcome 2

Synthesise and evaluate current trends in online and blended learning, teaching, and assessment.

Developing PATO required me to engage seriously with contemporary trends in online education. The course introduces participants to frameworks such as TPACK (Koehler et al., 2013), SAMR (Hamilton et al., 2016), Universal Design for Learning (CAST, 2024), and accessibility practices through curated resource collections (E10, E11). Rather than treating these frameworks as competing approaches, I found them useful for different purposes. UDL helped me think about learner variability and barriers, while TPACK gave me a way to consider how technology, pedagogy, and content fit together. When we looked at specific technologies in PATO, the question became much more practical: what does this tool add, what problems might it create, and is it accessible to the learners who need to use it? The shared emphasis was on deliberate integration, not novelty.

Our impact data showed participants reporting increased confidence in online instruction, improved course organization, and adoption of new technologies in their own teaching contexts. One participant noted the course "increased my confidence" and led to tools being used regularly afterward; another reported incorporating PATO strategies into fully online teaching. These results provide useful evidence that participants saw value in PATO and applied some of their learning afterward, but they do not tell me which parts of the course produced those changes (E14, E15, E18).

The PATO self-study surfaced a genuine tension: introducing participants to more technologies expanded awareness but also increased cognitive load and risked diverting attention from pedagogy. I now evaluate trends selectively, prioritizing those that address a specific learner need. I also acknowledge that PATO covers a selected subset, areas like learning analytics, digital citizenship, and culturally responsive pedagogy are relevant but weren't central to this program's scope.

Evidence Referenced: E10, E11, E14, E15, E18

Learning Outcome 3

Reflect on learning, teaching, and assessment strategies and evaluate their transferability to online and blended environment.

PATO reinforced for me that effective face-to-face teaching strategies cannot simply be moved online. They often need to be redesigned for the online environment. The onboarding sequence to the course (E03, E04, E05) is a clear example. Resources on Brightspace navigation and course orientation were developed specifically to reduce cognitive load and uncertainty, because online learners need support structures that aren't necessary in a physical classroom.

My earlier instinct was to translate existing materials into digital formats. PATO taught me that effective transfer requires redesign: online learners need more explicit orientation, clearer sequencing, and deliberately designed interaction built into the course architecture. The self-study and recommendations process (E14, E18) consistently pointed to scaffolding and learner readiness as areas requiring ongoing attention.

Evidence Referenced: E03, E04, E05, E14, E18

Learning Outcome 4

Design and evaluate a module, (with an appropriate mix of synchronous and asynchronous elements), for the online/blended environment, considering the technologies available to support the online learner.

PATO was designed as a blended learning experience that combined asynchronous preparation with synchronous learning, and follow-up activities. Participants completed focused pre-work before each live session, used the synchronous sessions to discuss and apply the ideas, and then had access to resources and reflection activities afterward. Detailed lesson plans (E17) show intentional alignment across outcomes, activities, technologies, and engagement strategies throughout the course (E01, E03–E09).

The blended structure worked well for flexibility and interaction, but it relied on participants completing pre-work before live sessions. When preparation was incomplete, the synchronous component couldn't function as designed. This taught me to keep preparatory work tightly focused, make its purpose explicit, and ensure live activities remain accessible without simply repeating the asynchronous content.

Evidence Referenced: E01, E03-E09, E17

Learning Outcome 5

Deliver an online class, which incorporates teaching strategies to promote learner engagement and support different groups of learners.

PATO gave me the opportunity to develop and refine my approach to facilitating learning in an online environment. The synchronous sessions were designed around discussion, polling, collaborative activities, reflection, and opportunities for participants to connect course concepts to their own teaching practice (E08, E09, E17). Rather than using the live sessions primarily to present content, I used them to build on the asynchronous pre-work, answer questions, explore ideas in more depth, and give participants opportunities to learn from one another.

What became clear through facilitation was that online engagement requires more than simply providing interactive activities. Participants came to the sessions with different levels of preparation, confidence with technology, and willingness to participate. When learners had not completed the pre-work, or when an activity required too much explanation or technical setup, it could quickly affect the pace and value of the session. This taught me to be more deliberate about what I ask learners to do before and during a synchronous class. Activities need to have a clear purpose, instructions need to be simple, and facilitators need enough flexibility to adjust when learners need additional support. The evaluation and impact evidence also reinforced that participants valued opportunities to interact with others and apply what they were learning to their own teaching contexts (E14, E15).

Evidence Referenced: E08, E09, E14, E15, E17

Learning Outcome 6

Develop an appropriate assessment strategy for an online or blended module that reflects best practice and provides mechanisms for feedback.

Assessment in PATO was built alongside the learning design using constructive alignment as a guiding principle, considering the relationship among intended learning outcomes, participant activities, and evidence of achievement (Biggs & Tang, 2011). The strategy combined formative and summative assessments: module reflections, checklists, polls, discussion activities, and exit tickets created lower-stakes opportunities to articulate understanding and receive feedback. The final Online Instructor Readiness Evaluation asked participants to apply their learning to an actual or planned online course, shifting assessment from recall toward authentic design decisions (Wiggins, 1998).

I used these assessment approaches for different reasons. Polls and exit tickets were particularly useful during facilitation because they gave me a quick sense of whether participants were following the ideas or whether something needed more attention. Discussions allowed participants to compare how they might approach the same problem, while the module reflections gave them space to connect what they were learning back to their own teaching. The final assessment required participants to bring that learning together by applying it to an actual or planned online course. Feedback was embedded throughout rather than concentrated at the end, consistent with the principle that effective feedback helps learners recognize their current performance, understand an intended standard, and take action (Nicol & Macfarlane-Dick, 2006).

It's worth distinguishing participant assessment from course evaluation. The exit survey (E16), impact infographic (E15), self-study (E14), and recommendations report (E18) gathered information about course effectiveness and transfer, they're program evaluation tools, not assessments of individual learning outcomes. Reflections, discussion contributions, and the final readiness evaluation were the actual learning assessments.

Evidence Referenced: E07, E09, E14, E16, E17, E18

Learning Outcome 7

Evaluate and appraise approaches for integrating group work and collaboration can be integrated into online/blended class.

Collaboration was a core design principle in PATO. Community Guidelines (E06) set expectations for respectful interaction, while synchronous sessions incorporated collaborative mind-mapping, small-group discussions, and peer learning activities (E09, E17). These were designed to support social learning and professional networking, not just as activities for their own sake.

Collaborative work gave participants a way to compare practices and develop ideas with peers. The challenges were real, though: uneven participation, differing confidence levels, time constraints, and varying familiarity with the tools. Through facilitating these activities, I found that online collaboration worked best when learners knew exactly what they were being asked to do and why they were doing it. Group size mattered, as did clear facilitation and giving participants time afterward to reflect on what they had learned from the discussion.

Evidence Referenced: E06, E09, E17

Learning Outcome 8

Select and evaluate appropriate hardware and software to maximize student engagement in the online/blended class.

PATO required selecting, implementing, and evaluating multiple educational technologies. Brightspace served as the central LMS; Microsoft Teams supported synchronous sessions; and tools like Padlet, Mentimeter, Poll Everywhere, LinkedIn Learning, and Microsoft 365 were selected to serve specific pedagogical objectives (E03, E04, E10, E13). Technology selection was guided by learner experience, accessibility, flexibility, institutional support, and pedagogical alignment, not hype or convenience.

Hardware was a more significant factor than it might initially appear. Reliable audio, a stable connection, and the ability to manage multiple interaction channels all shaped the facilitation experience. A second screen helped separate presentation from participant monitoring; on a single device, activities needed to stay manageable. I evaluated hardware not just for technical access but for equitable participation, designing around commonly available institutional technologies and providing alternatives like asynchronous pre-work, session recordings, and chat for participants where real-time engagement wasn't practical (E15, E16, E18).

Curated resources (E10, E11) introduced participants to evaluation frameworks like TPACK and SAMR, encouraging critical assessment of tools rather than uncritical adoption. Impact data showed positive perceptions of technology integration and increased digital confidence. Evaluation reports also flagged key considerations around digital skill development, learner workload, and the need for technology support.

The main learning from this outcome: software and hardware can't be evaluated in isolation. A tool may be pedagogically sound but still create barriers if it assumes technical capacity or equipment that participants don't have. I now ask more questions before introducing a new technology. Is it accessible? Is it supported at SAIT? What hardware does it require? How much time will learners need just to figure out how it works? Most importantly, does it add enough to the learning experience to justify that additional complexity?

Evidence Referenced: E03, E04, E10, E13, E15, E16, E18

Conclusion

This portfolio demonstrates prior learning developed through my contribution to the design, facilitation, evaluation, and continued improvement of PATO. The evidence shows that my learning goes beyond producing a Brightspace course: it includes connecting theory with design decisions, adapting teaching strategies for digital environments, building synchronous and asynchronous learning experiences, developing assessment and feedback processes, supporting collaboration, and evaluating hardware and software in relation to learner needs.

The most significant shift in my thinking has been from viewing online teaching as content transfer to understanding it as the intentional design of a complete learner experience. Course organization, orientation, social presence, technology selection, and assessment aren't separate additions, they work together, and each can either support or obstruct participation and learning. The PATO evidence demonstrates that participation does not necessarily demonstrate learning, using a technology does not mean that it improved the learning experience, and giving learners access to something does not necessarily make the experience inclusive. On that basis, I believe this portfolio provides evidence of achieving the eight learning outcomes for LEAR09046 through sustained professional practice and critical reflection.
